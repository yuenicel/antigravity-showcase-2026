Sixteen years of bidding results reveal how zero vehicle population growth, the 10-year deregistrations cycle, and an unprecedented government “cut-and-fill” supply injection created the world’s most expensive car-ownership permits.

On a sunny Wednesday afternoon in October 2023, a silent bid processed through the Land Transport Authority’s automated auction terminal established a new economic reality for Singapore: a Certificate of Entitlement (COE) for a Category B passenger car cleared at an all-time record of S$150,001. A piece of paper granting ten years of driving rights now cost more than an entire three-room flat in many Southeast Asian metropolises—before the purchaser spent a single dollar on the vehicle itself.

That peak was neither a statistical fluke nor an overnight speculative bubble. It was the mathematical culmination of a 16-year structural squeeze born of Singapore’s uncompromising urban doctrine: zero net growth in private passenger car populations, strictly calibrated vehicle quotas tied to past 10-year deregistrations, and an influx of international capital and premium electric mobility brands that transformed automotive transport into a hyper-luxury asset class.

“When quota supply collapsed below 500 units a round in late 2023, the market entered uncharted price-inelastic territory. Buyers weren’t bidding for a car—they were bidding for one of Singapore’s rarest legal permissions.”

An exhaustive investigation of 2,000 official bidding rounds from January 2010 through November 2026 demonstrates how the COE mechanism shifted from a progressive market-clearing rationing tool into an economic crucible.

The Scissor Squeeze: Cat A vs. Cat B Inversion

Historically, Singapore’s vehicle quota system maintained a clear social and economic boundary. Category A was reserved for mass-market buyers—families purchasing modest Japanese sedans or compact hatchbacks under 1,600cc. Category B accommodated luxury sedans, European SUVs, and high-performance engines. For over a decade, Cat B commanded a persistent 20% to 40% premium spread over Cat A.

However, the data reveals an extraordinary convergence in late 2026: Cat A cleared at $130,100, while Cat B cleared at $125,600—a total inversion of the historical premium ladder. How did modest family car permits become more expensive than luxury permits?

The culprit was twofold. First, automotive manufacturers systematically recalibrated electric vehicle drivetrains specifically to slide under the 110kW threshold required for Cat A classification. Second, ride-hailing fleets and private car-sharing syndicates aggressively targeted Cat A allocations to renew aging operational fleets, crowding out traditional household buyers with corporate balance sheets.

The Intervention: The 2023 “Cut-and-Fill” Relief Valve

By mid-2023, with Category B premiums breaking through $140,000 and the Open Category crossing $150,000, public frustration reached an inflection point. Because Singapore’s vehicle quota formula was strictly tied to deregistrations over the preceding four quarters, the market had entered a 10-year demographic valley corresponding to the low-supply period of 2013–2014.

Recognizing that waiting for the organic 2025–2027 deregistrations peak would cause severe economic distortions, the Ministry of Transport unveiled its landmark “cut-and-fill” policy. The LTA brought forward guaranteed future quotas from the anticipated 2025–2027 supply wave and injected them directly into 2023 and 2024 bidding cycles.

The empirical effect was immediate. Cat B quotas expanded from an all-time low of 472 units in October 2023 to more than 950 units by late 2026, putting an immediate brake on runaway bidding escalations and stabilizing prices between $120,000 and $130,000.
